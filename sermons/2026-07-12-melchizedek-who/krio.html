Melchizedek Who?Melchizedek na Udat?

Hebrews 7:1–10 · English & Krio (Sierra Leone)
About this version. Each English paragraph is followed by a Krio translation (shaded) so readers can follow along in either language. The Krio uses an everyday, anglicised spelling for easy reading. If you would prefer the formal Krio Bible spelling (as in Di Gud Nyus, with ɛ ɔ ŋ), that can be provided. A quick check by a native Krio speaker is recommended before public use.

IntroductionBigin

I guarantee you’ve never heard of Vasili Arkhipov. But he’s a really important person.

A promis yu, yu neva yeri bot Vasili Arkhipov bifo. Bot i na wan pesin we impotant bad.

He was a nuclear submarine commander for the Soviet Union (Russia) back in the 1960s.

Bak na di 1960s, i bin na di komanda fo wan nyuklia submarin — di kayn bot we de travel ondanit di wata — fo di Soviet Union (Rushia).

He’s famous because he stopped the other commanders from launching a nuclear missile at the United States from their position in the Caribbean.

Pipul sabi am bikos i bin stop di oda komanda dem we bin want fo fayr wan nyuklia misail go mit America, di tem we dem ol bin de na di Caribbean.

He felt there wasn’t the warrant to do such a thing. Legend has it he swallowed one of the keys needed to launch the missile so it couldn’t be done.

I bin fil se dem no get gud rizin fo du dat kayn tin. Pipul de tok se i bin swala wan pan di ki dem we dem nid fo fayr di misail, so dat nobodi no ebul du am.

No one really knows his name today – but he points us to how someone can have a great impact for peace when there could have been war.

Tide, nobodi rili sabi in nem — bot i sho wi aw wan pesin kin mek big diffrens fo pis, na di sem tem we war bin de fo bigin.

Melchizedek acts in much the same way. We may not have heard about him. But Hebrews 7 reminds us of him because Melchizedek shines a light not on himself – but on the greatness of the Lord Jesus – and how the Lord Jesus wins a greater peace and a greater salvation for his people.

Melchizedek de du di sem tin. Wi no bin yeri bot am, bot Hibru 7 mek wi memba am, bikos Melchizedek no de shayn layt pan insef — i de shayn layt pan di gretnes fo di Masta Jizos, en aw di Masta Jizos de win beta pis en beta salveshon fo in pipul dem.

Melchizedek is only mentioned in passing twice in the whole Old Testament — about three verses in Genesis (which we read) and one verse in Psalm 110. That’s it.

Na tu tem nomo di ol Old Testament tok small bot Melchizedek — bout tri vas na Genesis (we wi rid) en wan vas na Psalm 110. Na so nomo.

We all know babies called Joshua, Samuel, Mary. But no baby recorded in 2026 was given the name Melchizedek.

Wi ol sabi pikin dem we dem kol Joshua, Samuel, Mary. Bot na 2026, dem no rayt dong eni pikin we dem gi di nem Melchizedek.

Yet the writer to the Hebrews thinks Melchizedek is incredibly important.

Bot stil, di pesin we rayt di buk fo Hibru tink se Melchizedek impotant pas mak.

We know from chapter 5, and now again in chapter 7, that Jesus is a priest in the order of Melchizedek. And that only matters if we understand who Melchizedek is.

Wi sabi from chapta 5, en naw agen na chapta 7, se Jizos na prist na di layn fo Melchizedek. En dat only impotant if wi ondastand udat Melchizedek na.

Our whole series has been about how Jesus is better. One of the ways he’s better is that he’s a priest in the line of Melchizedek. That is one of the reasons Jesus is a better high priest for us.

Wi ol dis siriz na bin bout aw Jizos beta pas ol tin. Wan pan di we we i beta na se i na prist na di layn fo Melchizedek. Dat na wan pan di rizin dem we mek Jizos na wan beta hay-prist fo wi.

Again — why is the language of ‘high priest’ important? Listen to Hebrews 4:14–16:

Agen — wetin mek di tok bout ‘hay-prist’ impotant so? Lisin to Hibru 4:14–16:

Therefore, since we have a great high priest who has ascended into heaven, Jesus the Son of God, let us hold firmly to the faith we profess. For we do not have a high priest who is unable to empathize with our weaknesses, but we have one who has been tempted in every way, just as we are — yet he did not sin. Let us then approach God’s throne of grace with confidence, so that we may receive mercy and find grace to help us in our time of need.

“So den, siik wi get wan gret hay-prist we don go op na evin — Jizos, di Pikin fo God — mek wi ol tinap tranga na di feth we wi de profes. Bikos di hay-prist we wi get, i no na pesin we no ebul fil wetin wi de fil na wi wiknes; i na pesin we dem bin tempt am na ol kayn we, jos lek wi — bot i no sin at ol. So mek wi kam klos to God in tron we ful op wit gres, wit bol at, so dat wi go get mersi en fen gres we go ep wi na wi tem we wi nid am.”

Who our high priest is matters. Does he grant us access to God? Does he understand, as our representative, the human condition — what it is like to live in a fallen world with sinful hearts? Will we find grace and mercy from God through him? Will we find what we need to keep us going in the Christian life?

Udat wi hay-prist na, dat impotant. I de opin di do fo wi go mit God? As di pesin we de tinap fo wi, i ondastand aw wi layf tranga — aw i tan fo liv na dis wol we don spoyl, wit at we get sin? Wi go fen gres en mersi from God tru am? Wi go fen wetin wi nid fo mek wi kip de waka na di Kristian layf?

Jesus does all of that. Sounds good, right?

Jizos de du ol dat. I sweet fo yeri, no so?

But of course, for the audience — these Jewish-background believers — all they had ever known was the priesthood of the Levites. The Levitical order. What they had known and heard about for a thousand years.

Bot fo tru, fo di pipul we bin de yeri — dis Jew-bakgraun biliva dem — na di prist biznes fo di Levayt dem nomo dem bin sabi. Di Levayt layn. Na dat dem bin sabi en yeri bout fo wan tousin ia.

Were they tempted to return there? To go back to what they knew? But to return there is to go back to a priesthood that cannot achieve what Jesus achieves.

Dem bin de fil fo go bak de? Fo go bak to wetin dem sabi? Bot fo go bak de, na fo go bak to wan prist biznes we no ebul du wetin Jizos de du.

He is of a different order. He is a priest in the order of Melchizedek. Qualitatively better. To grasp that, we need to see who Melchizedek is.

I na wan difren kayn. I na prist na di layn fo Melchizedek. I beta na in ol we. Fo ondastand dat, wi nid fo si udat Melchizedek na.

1. Melchizedek resembles Jesus (7:2–3)1. Melchizedek tan lek Jizos (7:2–3)

There are three ways Melchizedek’s greatness is shown from Genesis 14.

Na tri we di gretnes fo Melchizedek de sho, from Genesis 14.

Background: Genesis 14. Abraham, it turns out, is not just a farmer. There is war in the region — many kings fighting each other. His nephew Lot is captured by some of these kings, who also grab plunder from the area around Sodom.

Di bakgraun na Genesis 14. I kam sho se Abraham no na jos wan fama. War de na di area — boku king dem de fet dem kompin. Dem grab in nevyu Lot — na som pan dis king dem tif am — en dem tif boku tin (plonda) bak from rawnd Sodom.

So Abraham plans an amazing rescue: he takes 318 trained men, defeats this coalition of kings, and recovers Lot and much of the plunder that had been taken.

So Abraham plan wan gbana reskyu: i tek 318 sojaman we dem tren, i biit dis gang fo king dem, en i get bak Lot en boku pan di tin dem we dem bin tif.

And as he is returning home, suddenly, out of nowhere, comes this strange figure: Melchizedek.

En as i de go bak na os, wantem, from nowe, dis strenj pesin kam bifo am: Melchizedek.

He is a king from Salem — most likely Jerusalem. He is a priest. Not just any priest — a priest of the one true God.

I na king from Salem — mo lek na Jerusalem dat. I na prist. No na jos eni prist — i na prist fo di wan tru God.

He brings bread and wine. He blesses Abraham — and Abraham, in return, gives Melchizedek a tenth of the spoils he is bringing back.

I bring bred en wayn. I bles Abraham — en Abraham, fo pe am bak, gi Melchizedek wan-tent pan ol di tin dem we i de bring bak.

As quickly as he arrives, he is gone. We hear nothing else about him. Random? No — nothing is random in God’s plan.

Di sem kwik we i kam, na so i go. Wi no yeri notin agen bout am. I jos apin so? No — notin no de apin jos so na God in plan.

The writer to the Hebrews zeroes in on a few things that reveal he is not just an ordinary man. Read verses 2–3. Biblical names are important: God has arranged all these characters and their names to tell us something.

Di pesin we rayt Hibru fokos pan som tin dem we sho se Melchizedek no na jos wan ordineri man. Rid vas 2–3. Di nem dem na di Baybul impotant: God don arenj ol dis pipul en di nem we dem get fo tel wi somtin.

Melchizedek’s name means ‘King of Righteousness.’ And he is King of Salem — in Hebrew the consonants sound like the letters for ‘Shalom’: peace. Not just peace between people, but world-wide peace. Harmony.

Melchizedek in nem min ‘King fo Raytiosnes’ (King fo di rayt-rayt we). En i na King fo Salem — na Ibru langwej, di leta dem de sawnd lek ‘Shalom’: pis. No na jos pis bitwin pipul, bot pis fo di ol wol. Ol man de gri wit dem kompin.

According to the Bible, two of the greatest things the world can have are peace between a warring world and God, and righteousness for a world lost in sin and wickedness. Here is a King of Righteousness and a King of Peace.

Akodin to di Baybul, tu pan di gretes tin dem we di wol kin get na: pis bitwin dis wol we de fet en God, en raytiosnes (fo liv rayt-rayt) fo wan wol we don los na sin en bad-at. Naya wi si wan King fo Raytiosnes en wan King fo Pis.

But not the only one. The Bible looks forward to a figure to come who will not just point to these things but will embody them and pour them out on the world — Isaiah 9:6–7: the Prince of Peace, a Kingdom of Righteousness. Of course, we know that is found in the Lord Jesus.

Bot i no na di only wan. Di Baybul luk fowod to wan pesin we go kam — pesin we no go jos poynt to dis tin dem, bot go bi dem insef en por dem pan di wol — Aysaya 9:6–7: di Prins fo Pis, wan Kingdom fo Raytiosnes. Fo tru, wi sabi se na insay di Masta Jizos wi de fen dat.

Then the writer zeroes in on how Melchizedek is portrayed — in particular, on what we don’t hear about him.

Den di rayta fokos pan aw di stori sho Melchizedek — mo espeshali, pan wetin wi no yeri bout am.

One of Sherlock Holmes’s most famous cases turns on silence. There has been a mysterious break-in and murder in someone’s home. Who did it? A vital clue is that the murdered man had a dog in the house — and the dog was not heard barking on the night of the murder. The dog was silent.

Wan pan di mos fema kes dem we Sherlock Holmes solv, na bout wan tin we no mek noyz. Sombodi bin brok into wan pesin in os en kil am. Udat du am? Wan big klu na se di man we dem kil bin get wan dog na di os — en nobodi no yeri di dog de bak di nait we dem kil am. Di dog kwayet.

Why is that the vital clue? Holmes realises it must mean the dog knew the person who broke in and did the killing — that is why it did not bark. A vital detail. It is where we get the phrase ‘the curious incident of the dog in the night-time.’

Wetin mek dat na di big klu? Holmes andastand se dat fo min se di dog bin sabi di pesin we brok in en du di kilin — na dat mek i no bak. Wan big-big poynt. Na from de wi get di tok se ‘di kyurios tin bout di dog na di nait tem.’

What Genesis does NOT tell us about Melchizedek — what it is silent about — is used by Hebrews as a vital clue to how great he is, and to the one he points to.

Wetin Genesis NO tel wi bout Melchizedek — di tin we i kwayet bout — na dat Hibru tek as wan big klu fo sho aw i gret, en fo sho di wan we i de poynt to.

He notices that Melchizedek has no genealogy. Striking! Genesis is full of genealogies — everyone is connected to someone, each person the son or daughter of so-and-so. But with Melchizedek, we get nothing. Almost as if he has no mother or father.

I noti se Melchizedek no get eni famili layn (jenealoji). Dis wan sabraiz yu! Genesis ful wit famili layn dem — everibodi konekt to sombodi, everi pesin na di pikin fo so-en-so. Bot fo Melchizedek, wi no get notin. I tan lek se i no get mama o papa.

And the fact that he simply comes and goes — it is almost as if he is someone with no beginning and no end. We love origin stories; most movies these days are prequels showing where a character came from. Not so here — which is the key.

En di we aw i jos kam en go — i tan lek se i na pesin we no get bigin en no get en. Wi lek stori bout aw tin bigin; nowadays plenti muvi na prikwel we de sho usay wan karakta komot. Bot no so ya — en na dat na di ki poynt.

The narrative portrays Melchizedek as ‘always living’ (v.8). And if he is a priest, then he is a priest forever — almost like the Son of God himself. Is Melchizedek actually Jesus, in a pre-incarnate form? We don’t need to go there. The point is that Melchizedek is presented as holding a priestly office forever.

Di stori de sho Melchizedek lek pesin we ‘de liv oltem’ (v.8). En if i na prist, den i na prist fo eva — i tan olmos lek di Pikin fo God insef. Melchizedek na Jizos fo tru, bifo i bin bon lek man? Wi no nid fo go de. Di poynt na se di stori de sho Melchizedek lek pesin we get di prist wok fo eva.

Great news! Why? Because we need a priest who lives forever. Think how little lasts today — football managers sacked even during the World Cup; think how many prime ministers we have had here in the UK. Andy Burnham would be the UK’s seventh prime minister in ten years.

Gud nyus! Wetin mek? Bikos wi nid wan prist we de liv fo eva. Tink bout aw notin no de las tide — dem de sak futbol manija dem ivin insay di Wol Kop; tink bout aw plenti praym minista wi don get ya na di UK. Andy Burnham go bi di UK in sevent praym minista insay ten ia.

A sense of upheaval. There is no security when there is always change at the top. Not so with a priest like Melchizedek — and with whoever is a priest in his line.

Everi tin de shek. No sekyuriti de we chenj de apin oltem na di top. Bot no so fo wan prist lek Melchizedek — en fo enibodi we na prist na in layn.

Just as Melchizedek’s priesthood is presented in Genesis as that of a priest who is eternal, so Jesus’ priesthood, in Melchizedek’s line, is ACTUALLY eternal. Massive!

Jos lek aw Genesis sho Melchizedek in prist wok lek wan prist we de las fo eva, na so Jizos in prist wok, na di layn fo Melchizedek, na FO TRU i de las fo eva. Dis wan big bad!

Access is forever open. Grace and mercy are ours forever. Nothing can change that. A forever peace and a forever righteousness can be looked forward to.

Di do de opin fo eva. Gres en mersi na fo wi fo eva. Notin no ebul chenj dat. Wan pis we de fo eva en wan raytiosnes we de fo eva — na dem wi de luk fowod to.

Melchizedek is great. He is all about what our world needs: peace, righteousness, and eternal access to God. And because Jesus comes from his line — not Levi’s — that is what Jesus gives us, but in full. Melchizedek is the trailer; Jesus is the whole movie. So do not turn away from Jesus.

Melchizedek gret. I na bout ol wetin wi wol nid: pis, raytiosnes, en di we fo mit God fo eva. En bikos Jizos komot na in layn — no na Levi in layn — na dat Jizos de gi wi, bot ful-ful. Melchizedek na di trela; Jizos na di ol muvi. So no ton yu bak pan Jizos.

2. Melchizedek trumps Abraham and the Levites (7:4–10)2. Melchizedek beta pas Abraham en di Levayt dem (7:4–10)

When I first went to the job centre after university, one thing they tested was how fast I could type. You know the QWERTY keyboard — named after the top row of letters, dating back to the 1870s on typewriters. The layout is actually odd: the common letter pairs are separated, deliberately, to make people type slowly so they would take more care.

Di fos tem we A go na di job senta afta yunivasiti, wan tin we dem tes na aw kwik A kin tayp. Yu sabi di QWERTY kibod — dem nem am afta di leta dem na di top row, en i komot from di 1870s na di taypraita dem. Di we aw dem arenj di leta dem strenj: dem separet di leta dem we de kam togeda oftin, wit pupos, so dat pipul go tayp slo en tek mo tem-tem.

Much later, someone designed a new layout that put the most common letters on the middle row — less finger movement, faster typing, less strain. On a modern computer you can switch to it, but almost no one does, even though it is far better. Why? Because we prefer what we know. We return to what is familiar, even when there is no good reason.

Long afta, sombodi mek wan nyu leyawt we put di leta dem we pipul de yuz pas ol pan di midul row — so yu finga no de muv boku, yu de tayp kwik pas, en yu finga no de taya. Na modin kompyuta yu kin swich to am, bot olmos nobodi no de du am, ivin do i beta bad. Wetin mek? Bikos wi lek wetin wi sabi. Wi de go bak to wetin wi don yuz to, ivin we no gud rizin de.

The writer to the Hebrews knows his readers are finding life hard. People do not want them to be Christians. Jesus is mocked. So they are tempted to return to what they know: the Levitical order — the priesthood with the sacrifices, the temple, the robes, the altars. Familiar. But that system is worse for them.

Di pesin we rayt Hibru sabi se in pipul de fays tranga layf. Pipul no want mek dem bi Kristian. Dem de mek gem pan Jizos. So dem de fil fo go bak to wetin dem sabi: di Levayt layn — di prist biznes wit di sakrifays dem, di tempul, di klos dem, di alta dem. I familia to dem. Bot dat sistem wos fo dem.

The writer wants them to see: what you are used to may be familiar, but to pull back from Jesus is the worst thing you can do. Don’t look back to the Levites — the Old Testament priesthood. The order of Melchizedek has always been greater.

Di rayta want mek dem si: wetin yu don yuz to kin bi familia, bot fo pul bak from Jizos na di wos tin we yu kin du. No luk bak to di Levayt dem — di Old Testament prist biznes. Di layn fo Melchizedek na oltem bin gret pas.

How do we know? Melchizedek’s greatness over Abraham and the Levites — two points from Genesis 14.

Aw wi sabi? Di gretnes fo Melchizedek ova Abraham en di Levayt dem — tu poynt from Genesis 14.

(a) Melchizedek collects a tithe from Abraham(a) Melchizedek kolekt tayt from Abraham

Read verses 4–6a. The point is simple. It does not just say Abraham gives Melchizedek a tenth of the spoils he has won. It says Abraham — the patriarch — gives Melchizedek a tenth.

Rid vas 4–6a. Di poynt izi. I no jos tok se Abraham gi Melchizedek wan-tent pan di tin dem we i win. I tok se Abraham — di gran-gran papa fo di neshon — gi Melchizedek wan-tent.

Abraham is the father of the Jewish nation, the spiritual father of all believers. Even you and I trace our spiritual ancestry all the way back to Abraham. He is the one who really gets God’s plan of salvation going; his faith is the blueprint for ours; he receives all the promises. It is hard to think of a bigger spiritual ‘cheese’ than Abraham.

Abraham na di papa fo di Jew neshon, di spiritual papa fo ol biliva dem. Ivin yu en mi, wi spiritual famili layn de go ol di we bak to Abraham. Na in rili mek God in salveshon plan bigin; in feth na di patan fo wi own feth; na in get ol di promis dem. I tranga fo tink bout enibodi we big pas Abraham na spiritual tin.

And yet he pays a tithe to someone else — Melchizedek! Why? Because Abraham acknowledges Melchizedek’s greatness. That is the writer’s point in verses 5–6.

Bot stil, i pe tayt to sombodi els — Melchizedek! Wetin mek? Bikos Abraham gri se Melchizedek gret. Na dat na di rayta in poynt na vas 5–6.

It was normal to give tithes to the Levites in the Old Testament — but people did that not because they were overawed by the Levites, but because the law commanded it. It was how the Levites made their living without a formal income. Not so with Melchizedek: no law said Abraham must tithe to him. Abraham does it simply because he recognises Melchizedek’s greatness.

I bin normal fo gi tayt to di Levayt dem na di Old Testament — bot pipul no bin de du dat bikos di Levayt dem bin de sabraiz dem, bot bikos di lo tok se dem fo du am. Na so di Levayt dem bin de fen dem fud we dem no get propa pe. Bot no so fo Melchizedek: no lo tok se Abraham fo gi am tayt. Abraham du am jos bikos i sabi se Melchizedek gret.

And if Abraham tithed to him, then in a sense the Levites tithed to Melchizedek too — that is the logic of verses 9–10. We in the West don’t quite grasp that, being too individualistic; but it would make sense to most cultures in the world. You and your descendants are bound together; you come as one. If you thought the Levitical priests were impressive — they themselves thought Melchizedek was far greater!

En if Abraham gi am tayt, den na wan we, di Levayt dem gi Melchizedek tayt bak — na dat na di sens fo vas 9–10. Wi na di Wes no de ondastand dat wel, bikos wi de tink bout wisef nomo; bot i go mek sens to boku kolcha dem na di wol. Yu en yu pikin-pikin dem tay togeda; una kam as wan. If yu bin tink se di Levayt prist dem gbana — dem dem sef bin tink se Melchizedek beta pas dem bad!

(b) Melchizedek blesses Abraham(b) Melchizedek bles Abraham

Read verses 6b–7. Blessing here is not about praising someone. Here, and elsewhere in the Bible, it means declaring God’s blessing over someone — usually a spiritual leader blessing someone under him. You see it in Church of England services when a leader blesses someone who comes for a blessing at communion.

Rid vas 6b–7. Di ‘bles’ ya no min fo prez sombodi. Ya, en na oda ples na di Baybul, i min fo tok God in blesin ova sombodi — mostli na wan spiritual lida de bles sombodi we de ondanit am. Yu de si am na Church of England savis dem, we di lida de bles sombodi we kam fo blesin na komyunion tem.

Melchizedek’s blessing reminds Abraham who really won the victory over those kings: the Lord. It is a way of saying, ‘The Lord is with you and working through you, and I officially recognise and pronounce that to be true.’

Melchizedek in blesin mek Abraham memba udat rili win di viktri ova dem king de: na di Masta. Na wan we fo tok se, ‘Di Masta de wit yu en de wok tru yu, en A de ofishali gri en tok se na tru.’

So you see why it is the lesser who is blessed by the greater. Abraham — the patriarch, the man with the promises — is inferior to this priest. That priest is someone significant: more significant than Abraham, more significant than the Levitical priests. And Jesus is a priest in his line. What you might want to turn back to is inferior — so do not turn away.

So yu si wetin mek na di wan we sma de get blesin from di wan we big. Abraham — di gran-papa, di man we get di promis dem — sma pas dis prist. Dat prist na sombodi impotant: i impotant pas Abraham, i impotant pas di Levayt prist dem. En Jizos na prist na in layn. Wetin yu kin want fo ton bak to, i sma pas — so no ton yu bak.

3. Melchizedek’s combined office (v.1)3. Melchizedek get tu wok na wan (v.1)

One of Pixar’s geniuses is that it speaks to both adults and children. The animations entertain kids, but they are also packed with references adults love — Aladdin is full of pop-culture jokes children can’t even understand. To combine both is a rare skill.

Wan pan di gbana tin dem bout Pixar na se i de tok to big pipul en pikin dem, ol tu. Di katon dem de swit fo pikin dem, bot dem ful bak wit tin dem we big pipul lek — Aladdin ful wit joke dem bout kolcha we pikin dem no ivin ondastand. Fo mek di tu kam togeda, na wan skil we no komon.

The last point is the most obvious one from Melchizedek’s description. Verse 1: he combines something incredibly rare — he is both a King and a Priest. Rare, because in the Old Testament God had said a king could not be a priest; no one could do both. King Saul was punished for meddling with the priesthood. But in Melchizedek, the two are brought together — which again points us to Jesus.

Di las poynt na di wan we klia pas from aw dem tok bout Melchizedek. Vas 1: i put togeda somtin we no komon at ol — i na King en Prist, ol tu. I no komon, bikos na di Old Testament God bin tok se king no fo bi prist; nobodi no ebul du di tu. Dem bin ponish King Saul bikos i bin miks insef wit di prist biznes. Bot na Melchizedek, di tu kam togeda — en dat agen de poynt wi to Jizos.

Why does this matter? We will hear more next week, but only with Jesus do we have a High Priest who opens up access to God — who ministers grace and mercy to us when we are weak. And only with Jesus do we have a King who guarantees we will share in his victory over his enemies and ours.

Wetin mek dis impotant? Wi go yeri mo nex wik, bot na wit Jizos nomo wi get wan Hay-Prist we de opin di do fo mit God — we de gi wi gres en mersi we wi wik. En na wit Jizos nomo wi get wan King we de promis se wi go sher na in viktri ova in enemi dem en fo wi own.

Infinite mercy combined with infinite justice. Infinite grace combined with infinite power. Which means: with Jesus, we have it all. And only Jesus. Jesus is better in every way. When we see Jesus’ greatness, we are more likely to stay with him.

Mersi we no de don, put togeda wit jostis we no de don. Gres we no de don, put togeda wit powa we no de don. We min se: wit Jizos, wi get ol tin. En na Jizos nomo. Jizos beta pas na everi we. We wi si di gretnes fo Jizos, wi go mo redi fo tinap wit am.

Now, most of us here are probably not about to turn back to the Old Testament priesthood — we can’t anyway! But: where do you look to find peace — peace with God, peace with others, peace for the world — where do you look that is not Jesus? Only Jesus is the Prince of Peace.

Naw, boku pan wi ya, wi no de bout fo ton bak to di Old Testament prist biznes — wi no ebul, eniwe! Bot: usay yu de luk fo fen pis — pis wit God, pis wit oda pipul, pis fo di wol — usay yu de luk we no na Jizos? Na Jizos nomo na di Prins fo Pis.

Where do you look when you feel weak and sinful, when you feel you have let God down again — where can you find righteousness that is not just outside but inside too? Only Jesus is Righteousness itself; only Jesus has the righteousness we need and gives it to us.

Usay yu de luk we yu de fil wik en ful wit sin, we yu de fil se yu don disapoint God agen — usay yu kin fen raytiosnes we no de na di awtsay nomo bot na di insay bak? Na Jizos nomo na Raytiosnes insef; na Jizos nomo get di raytiosnes we wi nid, en i de gi wi am.

Where do you look when life feels chaotic — around you and inside you? When others let you down, when life throws a curveball, who gives you security? Only Jesus is the First and the Last, the Alpha and the Omega. Only with Jesus can we find a hope that never changes, a standing before God that can never change.

Usay yu de luk we layf tranga en everi tin de mari-mari — rawnd yu en insay yu? We oda pipul disapoint yu, we layf trowe somtin we yu no bin ekspekt, udat de gi yu sekyuriti? Na Jizos nomo na di Fos en di Las, di Alfa en di Omega. Na wit Jizos nomo wi kin fen wan op we no de eva chenj, wan tinap bifo God we no ebul chenj at ol.

Have you forgotten that Russian man’s name from the start? A forgotten person’s greatness is meant to make us see Jesus’ greatness. Hebrews 7 uses four verses in Genesis 14 to point to Jesus’ superiority in every way.

Yu don foget dat Rushia man in nem we wi bigin wit am? Di gretnes fo wan pesin we pipul foget, na fo mek wi si di gretnes fo Jizos. Hibru 7 yuz fo vas na Genesis 14 fo poynt to aw Jizos beta pas na everi we.

And here is the clue for keeping Jesus’ greatness in view: as we read the Scriptures more — and read them with the aim of knowing Jesus more — we will find so many treasures that help us behold him.

En naya di klu fo kip di gretnes fo Jizos na wi yay: as wi de rid di Baybul mo — en rid am wit di met fo sabi Jizos mo — wi go fen boku-boku gud tin we go ep wi fo luk pan am.
